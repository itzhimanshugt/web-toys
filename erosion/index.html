What each droplet does

A droplet starts on a random cell and rolls downhill, blending its old heading with the local slope by the inertia setting. Its sediment capacity is drop in height × speed × water × 4, with a small floor so it can still work on gentle ground.

Below capacity it scours a 3-cell-radius brush around itself. Above capacity, or when it climbs into a pit, it deposits. It loses water every step and stops after 30 steps.

Where the method comes from

The terrain is fractal Brownian motion (summed octaves of gradient noise) plus ridged noise, which folds each octave at zero to form sharp crests. The droplet scheme follows Hans Theobald Beyer's 2015 TU Munich thesis on hydraulic erosion.

Model time is a display scale only. Real rivers work far more slowly: the Colorado River cut the Grand Canyon, about 1.8 km deep, over roughly the last 5 to 6 million years.

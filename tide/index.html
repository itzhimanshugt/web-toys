How the machine works

Each constituent is a cosine, height = A cos(ωt − g), with ω = 360° divided by its period. On the machine, a crank turns at that speed; the radius of its pin sets the amplitude A and its starting angle sets the phase g. A slotted yoke turns the pin's circle into straight up-and-down motion, and a single wire passing over a pulley on every yoke adds all the motions together and moves the pen.

M2 and S2 drift in and out of step every 1/(1/12 − 1/12.4206) hours, about 14.77 days. In step they give spring tides; half a cycle later they partly cancel to give neaps. The form factor F = (K1 + O1)/(M2 + S2) classifies the result: under 0.25 semi-diurnal, 0.25 to 1.5 mixed mainly semi-diurnal, 1.5 to 3 mixed mainly diurnal, above 3 diurnal.

William Thomson designed the first tide-predicting machine in 1872-73. Presets here use equilibrium-tide amplitude ratios scaled so the largest constituent is 1 m, with all phases at 0°; they are not data for any port. Dial changes affect only the ink still to come, as on the real machine; a preset loads fresh paper.
